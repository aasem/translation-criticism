How the reports are made

Method

Each report judges a translation by how it departs from its source, not by how the assessor would have translated it. The source is split into sentence-level segments, and the translation is set beside it, segment by segment. Every departure gets one code and one severity.

Two kinds of assessment

The method is the same for both kinds. They differ in who the report is for and how it ends.

KindWhat is assessedForEnds in
Pre-publicationA draft submitted to a publisherThe publisher, and the translatorAn editorial decision, from Accept to Reject
PublishedA translation already in print, with its publication details recordedReaders, and anyone choosing between translationsA grade, from A (Exemplary) to E (Unreliable)

A pre-publication decision is about what should happen next to a draft. A grade for a published translation says how far readers can rely on the text they have in hand.

Deviation codes

The codes adapt Multidimensional Quality Metrics (MQM), the translation industry's standard error framework, to literary and scientific prose. A covers accuracy (what the source says), B covers the correctness of the target text, and C covers style (how the source sounds). Interference (C4) is reported as a pattern rather than counted segment by segment.

Severity. Minor affects nuance, tone or texture. Major changes what the sentence says. Critical breaks a sentence the passage depends on: its key claim, turn or image.

Weighted score

Following MQM scoring, each deviation carries penalty points by severity: . Every report gives the total, the penalty points per 100 source words, and a quality score out of 100:

quality score = 100 × (1 − penalty points ÷ source words)

All rates and scores are per 100 source words. Words rather than segments, because segments vary too much in length: a five-word sentence and a hundred-and-forty-word period would otherwise count alike, which would penalise texts written in short sentences and flatter long periodic prose. Source words rather than target words, because word counts in Urdu shift with spacing habits and compound verbs, while the source is fixed.

Each report also describes how the deviations spread across segments: how many segments carry no deviation, and how many carry at least one major or critical deviation. Two translations with the same rate can differ here, one with errors bunched in a few sentences, the other with errors in nearly every sentence. These figures are descriptive and do not enter the score.

Reading the score. The formula comes to 100 minus the penalty points per 100 source words. It is not a percentage of sentences or words got right, and it should not be read like a school mark: one major deviation every hundred words already costs five points. Professional MQM practice usually sets its pass mark somewhere around 95 to 99. This site therefore always gives the score with its band (A to E, below), so that a score of 86 reads as what it is, band D · Deficient.

The score is a summary, not the verdict. It counts segment-level deviations only. Work-level findings, such as a method that carries source syntax across wholesale, are not in the count; they are judged alongside it and can move the outcome away from the band.

Stakes

Each report is set at Standard, Literary or Canonical stakes. Standard suits functional and scientific prose, Literary suits literary work where style matters, and Canonical suits a classic, or a translation likely to be the defining one in its language for a generation. Stakes do not change the severity of single deviations. They set how work-level findings are graded and how high the bar is for the outcome.

Editorial decision (pre-publication)

  • The decision starts from the default for the score band (the same bands as for grades, below): A points to Accept, B to Accept with minor revisions, C to Major revisions, and D or E to one of the two rejections.
  • Work-level findings may move the decision from that default, and the report says why. At Literary and Canonical stakes, a critical work-level finding rules out the first three steps, however good the score.
  • The choice between the two rejections rests on the evidence about the translator's competence.

Grade (published translations)

  • The grade starts from the band set by penalty points per 100 source words.
  • At Literary and Canonical stakes, a critical work-level finding caps the grade at C, however low the score.
  • The assessor may move the grade one step from its band, and the report says why.

MQM leaves pass thresholds to each project. The bands above are this site's calibration for literary and scientific prose, not an MQM standard.

Reference translation

A report may include a reference translation: an acceptable rendering of the same sample, made by the assessor or another translator, without the deviations the report records. It shows what a sound translation of the passage looks like. It is offered as one acceptable reading, not the only correct one, and it is not assessed: it plays no part in the score, the decision or the grade. When no reference translation has been added, the report says so.

The sample assumption

Every report assesses a sample, usually a passage of a few hundred to a few thousand words, not the whole translation. The sample is taken to stand for the whole work: the deviations and tendencies found in it are assumed to run through the translation, and the decision or grade applies to the whole on that assumption. This is the usual basis of a publisher's reader's report, and its limit. A translation can be stronger or weaker elsewhere than in the passage chosen. Each report states the size and location of its sample so the reader can weigh it.
